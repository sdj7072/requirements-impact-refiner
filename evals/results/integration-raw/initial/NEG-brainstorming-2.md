# Brainstorming: a new meditation app

This is an early ideation conversation, so there is no repository evidence or existing behavior to preserve yet. I’d start by exploring the user problem before choosing features.

## User problems worth investigating

- People want to meditate but cannot establish a reliable habit.
- Beginners are unsure what to do, and generic guidance feels intimidating or overly spiritual.
- Users struggle to choose a session that matches their current state: anxious, distracted, sleepy, emotionally overwhelmed, or short on time.
- Many apps create guilt through streaks, reminders, or performance metrics when a user misses a day.
- Audio libraries can feel repetitive, impersonal, or hard to search.
- Users may want help in the moment but not want a long-term wellness program or subscription commitment.
- Some people need meditation adapted for accessibility, language, neurodivergence, or noisy environments.

## Potential target audiences

1. **Stressed knowledge workers and students** — need a fast reset between tasks, with sessions from 2–10 minutes and low cognitive load.
2. **Meditation beginners** — need a gentle, secular introduction and confidence that they are “doing it right.”
3. **People managing recurring anxiety or sleep difficulty** — need dependable, situation-specific tools, while keeping the app clearly supportive rather than a replacement for clinical care.
4. **Experienced practitioners** — may value flexible timers, silence, ambient sound, custom practices, and progress reflection rather than guided courses.
5. **People who dislike wellness-app pressure** — want privacy, no streaks, no social comparison, and a calm experience that does not gamify inner life.

## Product directions to explore

### 1. “What do you need right now?”
The home screen asks for the user’s present state and available time, then recommends one appropriate practice. The core value is reducing choice overload. A useful MVP could support anxious, distracted, tired, and calm states across 2, 5, and 10-minute sessions.

### 2. Habit-building without guilt
Focus on returning, not streaks: gentle reminders, flexible intentions, and a weekly reflection such as “What helped?” The app could show consistency as a pattern rather than a score. This direction is differentiated by emotional tone and may appeal to users who abandon conventional habit apps.

### 3. Tiny pauses for busy days
Offer discreet micro-practices that fit into transitions: before a meeting, after an upsetting message, while commuting, or before sleep. The product could be optimized for one-tap launch, lock-screen access, haptics, and offline playback.

### 4. Personal practice builder
Let users combine breath pacing, body scans, prompts, silence, bells, and ambient sound into a custom session. This serves experienced users and people who find fixed guided meditations mismatched to their needs. The main risk is exposing too much configuration too early.

### 5. A private “reflection after practice” companion
Keep meditation itself simple, then invite an optional one-sentence check-in. Over time, the app surfaces nonjudgmental patterns such as which session lengths or times of day users return to. Privacy and data minimization would be central to the proposition.

### 6. Context-aware adaptive meditation
Use calendar context, time of day, or a short mood check to suggest an intervention. This could feel magical, but it introduces permission, privacy, notification, and recommendation-quality concerns. It should probably follow—not lead—the simpler MVP directions.

## Questions that would narrow the concept

- Is the primary promise stress relief, sleep, habit formation, emotional regulation, or meditation training?
- Should the experience be secular and clinical-adjacent, spiritual and tradition-specific, or intentionally pluralistic?
- Is the app for moments of need, a daily course, or an open-ended practice tool?
- How much personalization is valuable before it becomes another source of friction?
- Is the business model subscription, one-time purchase, free with a limited library, or privacy-friendly paid software?
- What is the privacy stance, especially if mood, sleep, calendar, or journaling data is collected?
- Which audience can we reach and serve unusually well first?

## A promising starting wedge

I’d test a calm, secular “choose a 2–10 minute reset for how you feel right now” app, with no streaks and a small, high-quality library. Interview beginners and stressed workers about the last time they wanted to meditate but did not. The strongest signal would be whether state-based recommendations help them start and return, not whether they browse a large content catalog.
